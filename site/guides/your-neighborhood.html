Get involved in your neighborhood

Oregon's land use system requires every city and county to involve the public. Neighborhood groups are often where that starts.

Why Oregon has neighborhood groups

The first of Oregon's 19 statewide land use planning goals, Goal 1, requires each city and county to run a citizen involvement program, so residents can take part in every phase of planning. Cities meet it in different ways, and many rely on neighborhood associations or similar groups.

What it looks like where you live

  • Portland has 94 neighborhood associations, grouped into districts with district coalition offices, supported by the Office of Community & Civic Life.
  • Washington County replaced its former Community Participation Organizations (CPOs) in 2026. On January 27, 2026, the county board approved a new program it calls SPARC, Strengthening Participation and Reaching Communities. The county describes it as a district-aligned community engagement program, organized in four areas, one for each county commissioner district.
  • Many other cities, from Eugene and Salem to Beaverton and Bend, recognize neighborhood associations and notify them about land use applications nearby.

Search your city's website for "neighborhood associations." In rural areas, your county's planning department or a community planning organization is the place to start.

What neighborhood groups do

  • Get notice of land use applications and can comment, appear at hearings or appeal decisions.
  • Bring up traffic safety, parks, emergency preparedness and other local concerns with the city.
  • Run cleanups, events and small grants.

Getting started

  • Go to a meeting. Anyone who lives, owns property or works in the area can usually take part.
  • Watch for land use notices: the signs posted on a property and mailed notices to neighbors. They list the deadline to comment.
  • Know your city councilor. See who represents you.
