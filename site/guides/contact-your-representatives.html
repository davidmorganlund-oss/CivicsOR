How to contact the people who represent you

A short, specific message from someone who lives in the district gets read. Here is how to pick the right office and what to put in the message.

Start with the right level of government

The fastest way to get nowhere is to ask the wrong office. As a rough guide:

  • City: zoning and building permits inside city limits, city streets and sidewalks, city police, parks, water and sewer.
  • County: elections, property assessment and taxes, the sheriff and jail, the district attorney's office, public health, and land use outside city limits.
  • Metro: in the Portland area, the elected regional government handles the urban growth boundary, garbage and recycling, regional parks and supportive housing services.
  • Special districts: fire, water, parks, transit, ports and library districts each have their own elected board.
  • State: school funding, state highways, prisons, the Oregon Health Plan, unemployment insurance, the DMV, and state law in general.
  • Federal: Social Security and Medicare, veterans' benefits, immigration, passports, federal taxes, and the national forests and other federal land that cover much of Oregon.

Not sure who yours are? Use Who represents you.

What to put in the message

  1. Say you're a constituent. Give your city or town. Offices sort mail by whether you live in the district.
  2. Make one ask. "Please vote yes on House Bill 2001" is better than a general opinion. Include the bill or agenda item number if there is one.
  3. Give your reason in a few sentences. How it affects you, your family or your work.
  4. Keep it to a short page.
  5. Ask for a reply and include how to reach you.

Email, phone or in person

  • Email is the easiest to send and the easiest to track. Every legislator's address and Capitol phone number is on the Legislature's website.
  • A phone call is quick. Have your one ask ready. If you aren't sure who to call, the Legislature's information line is 1-800-332-2313.
  • Between sessions, legislators are home in their districts. Many hold town halls and coffee hours. That is often the best time to be heard, and the time to bring them an idea for a bill.
  • In person works best at the local level. See how to speak at a local public meeting.

When you need help, not a vote

If you're stuck with a government agency, elected officials' offices can often help. Members of Congress have staff who handle problems with federal agencies. State legislators can ask a state agency about your case. Call the office and ask for help with a constituent issue.
