The Oregon Constitution

Oregon's constitution is older than the state itself, and Oregon voters change it more often than most. Here is what it does and how it changes.

What it is

Oregon's constitution was approved by voters in 1857 and took effect when Oregon became a state on February 14, 1859. It sets up the Legislature, the governor and other statewide offices, and the courts, and it limits what state and local governments can do.

Parts worth knowing

  • Article I, the Bill of Rights. Free expression, freedom of worship, the rights of people accused of crimes, and more. Oregon courts sometimes read these protections more broadly than the U.S. Constitution's.
  • Article IV, the Legislature. Since 1902, it has also reserved to the people the power to propose laws and amendments by initiative and to refer laws to the ballot by referendum.
  • Article XI, local government and taxes. This is where the property tax limits voters added in the 1990s live, including the rule that property tax measures in elections other than May and November need at least half of registered voters to turn out.

How it changes

  • The Legislature can refer an amendment to the ballot. Voters must approve it.
  • Voters can propose an amendment by initiative. It takes signatures equal to 8 percent of the votes cast for governor at the last election, then a majority of voters. See how initiatives work.

Because amending is relatively easy, Oregon's constitution has been changed many times, and some of the most important rules about taxes and elections come from voter initiatives.

Read it yourself

The Legislature publishes the full current text, and the Oregon Blue Book has a short history.
