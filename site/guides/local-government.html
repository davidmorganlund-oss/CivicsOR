How local government works in Oregon

Counties, cities, Metro, and hundreds of special districts each run part of daily life. Here is who does what, and where you can step in.

Counties

Oregon has 36 counties. Each one runs elections, assesses property and collects property taxes, runs the jail through the sheriff, prosecutes crimes through the district attorney, handles public health, and plans land use outside city limits.

  • Most counties are run by a board of commissioners with three to five members. In a handful of mostly rural counties, a county court does the job: a county judge, who also runs county business, and two commissioners.
  • Nine counties have home rule charters, written and amended by their own voters. Multnomah County's charter, for example, sets up an elected chair and four district commissioners.

Cities

Oregon has more than 240 cities. A city council (in some cities, a commission) passes local laws, sets the budget, and hires a city manager or administrator in most cities. Cities handle zoning inside city limits, police, streets, parks, water and sewer.

Portland changed its government in 2025. The city now has 12 councilors, three elected from each of four districts using ranked-choice voting, and a mayor who runs city bureaus but doesn't sit on the council.

Metro

In the Portland area, Metro is a regional government elected by voters in the urban parts of Multnomah, Washington and Clackamas counties. It manages the urban growth boundary, garbage and recycling, regional parks and natural areas, the Oregon Zoo and the Oregon Convention Center, and the regional supportive housing services tax. Its council meets in public and takes testimony.

Special districts

Oregon has more than a thousand special districts, each with its own elected board: fire and rescue, water, parks and recreation, ports, transit, libraries, cemeteries, irrigation and more. School districts, education service districts and community colleges work the same way. These boards are covered by the Public Meetings Law, and their seats often go uncontested. They are a good place to get involved and to run for office.

Land use: Oregon's system is different

Since 1973, every Oregon city and county has had to plan under statewide land use goals, including citizen involvement. Each city sits inside an urban growth boundary that separates land for development from farms and forest. That makes local planning decisions matter.

  • Watch for notices. Neighbors near a proposed development get mailed notice of land use hearings, and notices are posted on the site.
  • Raise it, or you may lose it. If you want to appeal a land use decision to the state Land Use Board of Appeals later, you generally have to raise your issue, in writing or in testimony, before the local hearing record closes. Be specific.

Neighborhood groups

Many cities recognize neighborhood associations, and Washington County has Citizen Participation Organizations, or CPOs, that review land use proposals and talk with county staff. They are a low-key way to learn what's coming and to be heard early.
