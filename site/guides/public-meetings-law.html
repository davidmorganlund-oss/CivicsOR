Oregon's Public Meetings Law, in plain language

Notice, executive sessions, minutes, and what to do when a board breaks the rules.

Who it covers

The law (ORS 192.610 to 192.705) covers the governing body of any public body: city councils, county boards and courts, school boards, special district boards, state boards and commissions, and the committees, subcommittees and advisory groups that make decisions or recommendations to them. It applies whenever a quorum meets to decide or deliberate on public business, in person, by phone, online, or through a chain of emails or messages among members.

Notice

  • Every meeting needs notice that is reasonably calculated to reach the public and interested people, with the time, place and a list of the principal subjects the body expects to consider.
  • A special meeting needs at least 24 hours' notice. An emergency meeting can be called on less, but the minutes must say why.
  • Meetings must be held in the area the body serves, in a place accessible to people with disabilities, and not where discrimination is practiced.

Executive sessions

A governing body can meet in a closed executive session only for reasons the law lists, such as:

  • hiring a public officer or employee, or considering complaints about one;
  • negotiating to buy or sell real property, and labor negotiations;
  • talking with its lawyer about current or likely litigation;
  • reviewing records that are exempt from public disclosure.

The body must announce the legal reason. Members of the news media can usually attend executive sessions, though they may be told not to report on certain things. No final action or decision can be taken in executive session. The vote has to happen in public.

Minutes and recordings

Every meeting must have written minutes or a sound, video or digital recording that shows who was there, the motions and proposals, the results of votes and how each member voted, and the substance of the discussion. They must be available to the public within a reasonable time.

When a board breaks the rules

  1. File a written grievance with the public body within 30 days of the alleged violation. Include your name and contact information, the date, and what happened. Many cities and counties post a grievance form.
  2. The public body has 21 days to respond in writing, and must send your grievance and its response to the Oregon Government Ethics Commission.
  3. Then you can file a complaint with the Ethics Commission, which has enforced the Public Meetings Law since 2024. Members found to have violated the law can face penalties.
  4. You can also go to court. A decision made in violation of the law can be voided in some cases.

Before you file, it often helps to raise the problem directly with the clerk or recorder. Many problems are mistakes that are fixed the next time.
