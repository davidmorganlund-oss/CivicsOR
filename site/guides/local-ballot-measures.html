Local ballot measures: bonds, levies and local laws

Most of what's on an Oregon ballot in May is local: school bonds, fire district levies, library districts. Here is how to read them.

The kinds you'll see

  • General obligation bonds borrow money for buildings and equipment, such as new schools or fire stations. They're repaid by a property tax for a set number of years.
  • Local option levies add a property tax for operations, such as teachers, firefighters or library hours, for up to five years (up to ten for capital projects). Many are renewals of an existing levy.
  • New districts with a permanent tax rate, such as a library or fire district.
  • Local laws and charter amendments referred by a council or commission, or put on the ballot by voters through a local initiative or referendum.

Rules that shape them

  • Property tax limits. Measures 5 and 50 from the 1990s cap property taxes, so many local governments rely on levies and bonds that voters approve.
  • Double majority. A property tax measure on a May or November ballot needs a simple majority. At any other election, at least half of registered voters must also cast a ballot, so most tax measures go on May or November ballots.

Reading a measure

  • Your county voters' pamphlet has each measure's ballot title, an explanatory statement, and arguments for and against.
  • For a tax measure, look for the estimated rate per $1,000 of assessed value and how long it lasts.
  • Ask the district for its plans and past audits, and see how it voted to put the measure on the ballot. The Community Calendar lists the meetings where these decisions are made.

Putting a measure on the ballot yourself

Counties, cities and districts have their own initiative and referendum process. See initiatives and referendums and ask your county elections office.
