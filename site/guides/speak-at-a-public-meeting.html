How to speak at a local public meeting

What Oregon's Public Meetings Law gives you, what it doesn't, and how to use your two or three minutes well.

What Oregon law gives you

Oregon's Public Meetings Law (ORS 192.610 to 192.705) covers the governing bodies of cities, counties, school districts, special districts and state boards, and the committees and advisory groups that make recommendations to them.

  • You can attend. Meetings are open to the public, and a quorum can't meet in private to decide or deliberate, outside the narrow exceptions the law allows.
  • You get notice. Every meeting needs public notice, with the time and place and a list of the main subjects the body expects to take up. A special meeting needs at least 24 hours' notice.
  • You can attend from home. Under a 2021 law, governing bodies must reasonably give the public a way to attend by phone or video. Where they take testimony in person, they must also let people testify remotely or send written testimony by email.
  • You can record. Oregon generally requires everyone's consent to record a conversation, but the law makes an exception for public meetings and hearings of government bodies.

What it doesn't give you

The Public Meetings Law gives you the right to watch, not always the right to speak. A board doesn't have to take public comment at an ordinary meeting. Most city councils, county boards and school boards choose to, under their own rules.

Public hearings are different. When a law requires a hearing, people get to testify. That includes the yearly budget and most land use decisions. If you want to be sure of being heard, a public hearing is the place.

Before the meeting

  1. Find the meeting. The calendar lists what's coming up near you.
  2. Read the agenda. Find the item you care about. Note whether it is a public hearing, an action item, or a work session, where a board discusses but usually doesn't vote or take testimony.
  3. Check how to sign up. Many bodies want you to sign up ahead of time, especially to speak online. Multnomah County, for example, closes sign-up at 4 p.m. on the Wednesday before its Thursday meetings. The sign-up rules are usually on the agenda.
  4. Send written comments too. Written testimony goes into the record even if you can't stay, and it's the safest way to preserve an issue in a land use case.

At the meeting

  • Say your name and where you live.
  • Make one point, and make it early. Most bodies give two or three minutes. See build a two-minute pitch.
  • Speak to the whole body, not to staff or the audience. Officials usually won't answer questions during public comment, and that's normal.
  • Leave a copy of your remarks with the clerk or recorder.

After

Check the minutes or recording to see what happened. Follow up by email with the members. If the decision is still ahead, come back when it's on the agenda for action.
